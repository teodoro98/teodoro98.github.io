The biggest crypto heist in history: the Binance hack

The evening of 29 October 2026, hour by hour: how the attacker got Binance to sign the withdrawals, and where the funds went.

On 29 October 2026 Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, lost about $6.3 billion in one evening. According to Elliptic it is more than four times the previous record, the 2025 Bybit hack, and almost certainly the largest single theft of any kind in history.

I followed the story closely, starting from the posts on X by analysts and blockchain intelligence firms who reconstructed it live, hours before the official statements. There was a lot of information, fragmented and at times contradictory. So I put it in order.

Every piece of information carries its label, because not everything you read about an attack is worth the same:

verified on-chainconfirmed on the blockchain by at least two independent, reliable sources.

They didn’t steal the key: they took over the stamp office

Every movement of funds on a blockchain needs a valid digital signature. The blockchain doesn’t ask who you are: it only checks the stamp. That is why whoever controls the system that applies the stamps controls the money.

Binance says it found no evidence of a private key leak. According to analysts (TRM Labs, Blockaid, Elliptic) the attacker did not copy a key, but took control of the system that signs withdrawals: their requests came out “stamped” and, for the blockchain, they were valid. analysts’ hypothesis

On Ethereum the attacker used EIP-7702, a feature introduced in 2025 that lets a wallet delegate its behaviour to a contract. With a single signed authorization, the hot wallet “Binance 14” became a vacuum cleaner obeying only the attacker.

50 signatures, one valid.

The cleverest trick lies in the nonce, the sequence number of every wallet: an authorization is valid only if its number matches the wallet's at that moment. Binance 14 went through thousands of them an hour, impossible to guess. So the attacker had 50 authorizations signed, with 50 consecutive numbers: only one hit the mark, and 3 minutes and 24 seconds later a single withdrawal took $2.53 billion.

Where the money went

  • 45%frozen by Tether, Circle and the other stablecoin issuers.
  • 15%converted by the attacker into ETH and TRX, before the freezes.
  • 40%still in the stolen form: bitcoin, ether, XRP and SOL have no issuer that can block them.

The first freezes came 37 minutes after the main hit. The attacker was left holding about $3.5 billion. Based on Arkham data, values at the time of the theft.

Four days later

Update of 2 November.

  • Who did itOn 31 October the FBI, together with the Secret Service and the IRS, attributed the theft to the North Korean group "TraderTraitor", the same behind the Bybit hack. Back then the attribution took 5 days, this time 2.attributed by the FBI
  • Withdrawals reopenedOn 1 November, in nine and a half hours, $4.65 billion went out and $1.01 billion came in: according to Binance it is 2.1% of what it holds. So far, no bank run.claimed by Binance
  • Who paysBinance says it has covered 90% of the losses with its emergency fund (SAFU), the futures insurance fund and the company's own treasury.claimed by Binance
  • The proof of reservesThe 31 October snapshot says the numbers add up: for bitcoin, ether and USDT the reserves cover what is owed to customers. The full cryptographic verification is still missing.
  • Where the money isBetween $2.7 and $2.8 billion is frozen or recovered; the attacker still holds about 512,000 ETH, spread across more than a thousand addresses.verified on-chain

What it teaches

  1. Cryptography held.People and processes around the signatures gave way.
  2. Whoever controls who signs, controls the funds.The signing system must be protected like the money it moves.
  3. Only what has an issuer can be blocked.For everything else, speed is what counts: every minute is worth millions.

What we still don’t know

  • Who really signed. How the attacker obtained valid signatures remains the biggest question.
  • The technical details. Binance had promised them "within 48 hours": as of 2 November they have not arrived.
  • The post-mortem. The official account, from start to finish.
